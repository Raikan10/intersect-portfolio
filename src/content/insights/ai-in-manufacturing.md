Mid-size manufacturers have a lot in common wherever they are: several plants or lines, a sizeable export business, and software that grew up over decades. Planning in one place, dispatch in another, finance in a third, and spreadsheets filling the gaps.

The question we hear is where AI fits in a business like that.

## Start in the office

The quickest wins are usually away from the shop floor:

- **Sales and production reporting.** Leadership wants to see sales by plant, product and customer without waiting for someone to compile it. That can be built over the existing system, with plain-language questions on top, without replacing anything. For one multi-plant manufacturer, the [first working demo](/work/manufacturer-sales-mis/) ran six days after the first meeting.
- **Export documentation.** Invoices, packing lists and certificates across many export markets are repetitive and rule-based. They can be drafted automatically for a person to check.
- **Missed commitments.** A daily summary of what was promised and not yet done catches problems before customers notice.

## The shop floor is a different kind of project

Camera-based quality inspection, operator guidance and production planning are where the larger savings can be, because they affect cost of goods and rework rather than visibility. Vendors in this space publish strong results, including big reductions in defect rates and paybacks inside 18 months.

But these projects mean cameras on the line, capital spend and a procurement cycle. They are best done with a specialist vendor, and with clear reporting already in place so you can measure the before and after.

## Why the boring part matters

The most common problem in manufacturing is not a lack of systems. It is that the systems do not talk to each other. Connecting them is the foundation for everything else, and it is the part that a small team can do quickly.
