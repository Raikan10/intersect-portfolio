Coimbatore and the wider Tamil Nadu belt are full of mid-size manufacturers: pumps, motors, textiles, foundries, engineering components. Many of them export. Most run on a mix of an older ERP or custom software, Tally and a lot of spreadsheets.

The question we hear is where AI fits in a business like that.

## Start in the office

The quickest wins are usually away from the shop floor:

- **Sales and production reporting.** Leadership wants to see sales by unit, product and customer without waiting for someone to compile it. A reporting layer over the existing system, with a plain-language chat on top, can be built without replacing anything. For one multi-plant manufacturer on a legacy VB6 system, the [first working demo](/work/manufacturer-sales-mis/) ran six days after the first meeting.
- **Export documentation.** Invoices, packing lists and certificates across many export markets are repetitive and rule-based. An agent can prepare drafts for a person to check.
- **Missed commitments.** A daily summary of what was promised in email and not yet done catches problems before customers notice.

## The shop floor is a different kind of project

Camera-based quality inspection, operator guidance and production planning are where the larger savings can be, because they affect cost of goods and rework rather than visibility. Indian vendors in this space publish strong results, including big reductions in defect rates and paybacks inside 18 months.

But these projects mean cameras on the line, capital spend and a procurement cycle. They are best done with a specialist vendor, and with the reporting layer already in place so you can measure the before and after.

## Why the boring part matters

The most common problem in manufacturing is not a lack of systems. It is that the systems do not talk to each other. Planning lives in one place, dispatch in another, finance in a third. Connecting them is the foundation for everything else, and it is the part that a small team can do quickly.
