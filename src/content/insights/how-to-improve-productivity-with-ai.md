Most productivity advice is about people working harder or using better apps. In the businesses we work with, the bigger gains come from looking at how work moves between people.

## Count the steps, not the hours

Take one routine job, like turning a customer order into an invoice, and write down every step. Who receives it, where it is typed, who checks it, where it waits for approval, which system it goes into next.

When owners do this honestly, two things usually show up:

1. **The same information is typed more than once.** From WhatsApp into a notebook, from the notebook into Tally, from Tally into a spreadsheet for the weekly review.
2. **Most of the elapsed time is waiting.** For an approval, for a price list, for the one person who knows how it works.

Each re-typing step and each wait is a candidate for automation. Each one removed is time back for everyone involved, not just one person.

## Standard work and exceptions

The rule we use: automate what is completely standard, and send the exceptions to a person.

If 90% of orders follow the same pattern, a system can handle those end to end. The other 10% go to someone with the order, the customer history and the reason it was flagged. Writing that boundary down is the most important part of the build, because it is what stops a system from quietly producing wrong output.

## Why the tools are not enough

We have watched a team get better AI tools and produce the same output as before. The tools were fine. The problem was organisational: nobody owned the data, approvals still went through one person, and work still waited in the same places.

So the order is: fix ownership and approvals, remove the obvious steps, then add AI where the work is standard. Measure it in hours returned and errors avoided.
